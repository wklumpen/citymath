How we pay for Calgary

A plain-language, independent look at where the money comes from, where it goes, and how your tax bill is set.

Every number here comes from the City's published budget, tax rates and assessment roll. Start at the top and take it one step at a time.

Start here

A city does things, and a city owns things

Calgary is two things at once. It is a set of services: someone answers 911, drives the bus, clears the snow and picks up the garbage. And it is an enormous collection of stuff: roads, pipes, bridges, buses, fire halls and rec centres. We pay for both, but not in the same way, and the City keeps two separate budgets to do it.

The operating budget

What the city does

$4.6 billiona year to run services

Mostly people and running costs: police officers, firefighters, bus drivers, snow plows, lifeguards, the lights in the library.

At home, this is groceries, gas and the power bill.

Paid mostly from property tax and fees.

The capital budget

What the city owns

$3.7 billionthis year to build, buy and replace things

Things that last for decades: water pipes, roads, bridges, buses and trains, fire halls, rec centres.

At home, this is the house, the car and a new roof.

Paid mostly from borrowing, savings and grants.

The two are tied together. Everything the city builds has to be run, repaired and one day replaced, and the list of what we own gets longer every year.

$155 billionis what the City says it would cost to replace everything it owns today, mostly roads and pipes. That is roughly $100,000 for every person in Calgary.

So the question behind every budget is not only what we want. It is whether we can afford to keep what we already have. The rest of this page follows the money, then shows where your own tax bill goes and how the rate is set.

2 · Your bill

What does one home pay, and where does it go?

The City's tax is your home's assessed value times a rate. The province collects its own tax on the same bill. This is only the City's part.

Where this home's City tax goes in a year

Property tax is not tracked to a service, so this splits your bill the same way the shared pot is split above. Water is not on the list, and waste and recycling barely is, because your utility bill pays for them.

3 · The rate

Where does the tax rate come from?

The City does not pick a rate and see what it raises. It works the other way round: Council decides how much money the City needs from property tax, then divides that by the value of everything that can be taxed. The rate is whatever comes out.

The rate=Money needed from property taxTotal assessed value of all taxable property
Your bill=Your assessed value × the rate

Rates are often quoted in mills. A mill is a thousandth: 5 mills means $5 of tax for every $1,000 of assessed value.

To see what that does to a tax bill, take a made-up town of ten homes: Strong, Alberta. Step through what happens as the town changes.

One more step: Calgary cuts the pie in two first

Before any rate is set, Council decides how much of the pie comes from homes and how much from businesses. Each side then gets its own rate from its own total value. Today homes pay about of the pie. Move the split and see what it does to a home's bill.

How this works, and what it leaves out

The budget diagrams use the City's approved 2026 budget. Spending by service is from the City's open budget data, grouped into plain-language categories. Where the money comes from is from the budget document. Each service's own fees and other revenue are shown going to that service, because that is how the budget counts them. Outside the utilities this is bookkeeping, not a legal earmark. Property tax and other general revenue cover the rest. Money moved in and out of reserves is shown as one net amount. The $155 billion replacement value is from the City's 2026 Corporate Asset Management Plan.
